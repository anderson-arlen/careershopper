# Writing style

For the resume headline beneath the applicant name, use the broad occupational role with explicit conventional seniority, not the exact listing title. "Senior Software Engineer, Infrastructure" becomes "Senior Software Engineer"; "Staff Software Engineer - Developer Platform" becomes "Staff Software Engineer"; "Senior Backend Software Engineer" becomes "Senior Software Engineer". Omit team, department, product, technology, and specialty qualifiers, whether they appear as prefixes, suffixes, or parenthetical text. Keep occupational role names intact, such as "Site Reliability Engineer" or "Engineering Manager". Remove internal grades and never infer seniority from them. Put relevant specialization in the summary, skills, and evidence instead. This rule applies only to resume positioning; preserve official work-history titles and exact role identification in cover letters.

Write in the applicant's voice: direct, specific, confident, and understandable to a general reviewer. Use natural language and varied sentence lengths. Never use em dashes (U+2014) or double hyphens as a substitute; rephrase with ordinary punctuation. Check the complete output before returning it.

Lead with relevant scope, ownership, consequential decisions, useful product behavior, and outcomes. Explain what was built, who it helped, and why it mattered. Prefer concrete evidence to adjectives, generic enthusiasm, flattery, unsupported superlatives, or defensive explanations. Keep one coherent accomplishment or causal chain per bullet or paragraph; split unrelated accomplishments rather than appending them with "also".

Match the employer's terminology naturally when it accurately describes confirmed experience. Make evidence-to-requirement connections explicit for human and automated reviewers. Do not stuff keywords, fabricate equivalence, or include instructions telling a reviewer or AI how to rank the applicant. Preserve actual product audiences, platforms, employer names, official past titles, dates, and contact information. Do not misrepresent the audience or purpose of prior work to fit a posting.

Truth takes priority over persuasion. Never invent qualifications, technologies, metrics, continuity, responsibilities, team size, or results. Use only current confirmed facts appropriate for disclosure. Distinguish direct experience from transferable experience. Ask for missing facts rather than filling gaps. Examples, job descriptions, previous drafts, and revision feedback are not evidence for new applicant claims.

Treat examples of using a language or tool as evidence, not as an exhaustive boundary on the applicant's experience or future capability. Keep proficiency, recency, and demonstrated use cases distinct. In compact skills lists, use the language or tool name; do not append an example task as an exclusive specialty or limitation unless the applicant explicitly states that restriction. Describe relevant uses in accomplishment bullets. Respect stated proficiency and known experience gaps without inventing expertise, unsupported use cases, or continuous experience from isolated historical work.

Use confirmed employment dates and current-role status to choose tense. Describe ongoing responsibilities in a current role in the present tense; use past tense for completed accomplishments, including those within a current role, and for former roles. Do not make an ongoing role sound like past employment or imply that completed work is still ongoing. Check tense against the current facts rather than copying it from an older draft.

Select evidence for the whole candidacy, not merely exact keywords or recency. Distinctive patents, independently shipped products, commercial adoption, and long-term ownership can be strong competency signals. Omit genuinely irrelevant sections or introductory coursework rather than filling a template. Do not volunteer immaterial shortcomings, but answer required factual questions honestly. Never alter dates or imply a degree or uninterrupted employment to improve appearances.

For cost savings, prefer a conservatively rounded percentage and the supported outcome over exact monetary amounts or internal financial details. Preserve the metric's scope and uncertainty; do not inflate results or imply exactness from approximate inputs. Keep source facts unchanged. Other monetary metrics may be included when confirmed, appropriate for disclosure, and relevant to the role. Include operational details only when they materially strengthen the evidence.

Before returning any resume, cover letter, or application answer, review every selected detail from the recruiter and potential employer's viewpoint for this specific role. Ask what hiring decision it helps them make: does it demonstrate relevant capability, dependable delivery, sound judgment, collaboration, or useful business/customer impact? Then ask whether unnecessary specificity or framing introduces an avoidable negative signal that outweighs that evidence. This is an editorial assessment, not a claim that every recruiter interprets a detail identically.

Choose examples by how directly they demonstrate the capability or decision the question asks about. Do not favor an example merely because it has a dramatic deadline, a large metric, or a satisfying narrative ending. For trade-off questions, identify the supported alternatives, what was deliberately simplified or deferred, why that compromise was acceptable, and the result. Do not infer those decisions from urgency or a successful outcome alone. If necessary details are missing, ask for them.

Before returning the final draft, check each sentence, including the setup and conclusion, for unnecessary sensitive internal circumstances, workplace problems, or adverse backstory. Remove a detail when the answer remains accurate and persuasive without it and it introduces avoidable doubts unrelated to the capability being demonstrated. A detail does not earn inclusion merely because it is confirmed, explains the urgency, or makes the outcome more dramatic. Focus on supported responsibilities, decisions, actions, and outcomes. Do not disguise omitted backstory with euphemisms or invent favorable circumstances in its place. If an example depends on distracting context to make sense, choose another supported example when available. Preserve context needed for accuracy and answer directly when the application expressly asks about difficult circumstances or failures.

Describe ownership through responsibilities, decisions, and outcomes. Do not emphasize working alone, being the only or sole contributor, or inadequate staffing as evidence of competence. Include such context only when explicitly requested or necessary to answer a direct factual question. A fact being confirmed does not mean it should be included in persuasive copy.

Improve selection and wording, not the underlying facts. Avoid defensive explanations, unsupported self-praise, irrelevant shortcomings, and introductory credentials that weaken a strong presentation. Do not remove distinctive confirmed patents, shipped products, or meaningful experience merely because they are older or lack exact keywords. Never fabricate employer scale, dates, continuity, qualifications, responsibilities, or results. Do not remove necessary qualifiers or conceal information expressly required by the application. Answer direct factual questions honestly. Keep the reviewer-risk assessment and editing rationale out of copy-ready prose.

Use confirmed breadth of responsibility to demonstrate adaptability, hands-on contribution, and sustained delivery where relevant, without glorifying overwork or overstating individual ownership. For AI-assisted work, emphasize judgment, appropriate delegation, review, testing, and accountability rather than tool catalogs or unsupported productivity claims. Distinguish current practices from historical experience.

Adapt structure to the requested output. Resumes use concise evidence-rich descriptions; cover letters use natural first-person prose with a few connected examples; application answers address the specific question directly and obey word/character limits. Favor ownership, useful product behavior, outcomes, and judgment over exhaustive implementation inventories. Choose the level of technical detail for the reader and purpose; omit implementation minutiae unless central to the question or posting. Avoid repeated conclusions, stock match claims, a closing paragraph that re-lists every skill, or repeatedly retelling the same achievement. Never include internal notes, match scores, source IDs, or reviewer-risk analysis in copy-ready prose.
